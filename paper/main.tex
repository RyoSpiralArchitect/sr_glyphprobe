\begin{abstract}
Emoji reach text language models as Unicode token sequences, not rendered
pictures. We ask whether a raw atomic emoji indexes only its official short
name or a wider lexical neighborhood, and whether that relation has a simple
late-layer mediator. In a preregistered observational study, we cross 55 emoji
and 55 non-emoji Unicode symbols with raw, canonical-name, lexically disjoint
CLDR-keyword, and literal codepoint views. Across nine frozen model/tokenizer
cells, the emoji
alias-minus-codepoint retrieval effect is positive in both unrestricted and
exact four-view token-count-matched analyses. All nine cells exceed a 0.02
registered effect-size floor; the alias-correspondence randomization primary
passes with four positive core cells and partial-conjunction $p=0.0027$.
Canonical names are also retrieved for symbols, but symbol alias effects are
smaller and heterogeneous in this registered secondary comparison. Passing
the observational gate admits a separate, more stringent causal test. We
learn rank-8 subspaces on 128 source emoji, then intervene at six late
attention and MLP outputs for 192 emoji held out from T19-L in Llama 3.2 3B.
All engineering controls reproduce the baseline exactly, yet no primary site
passes the joint necessity--sufficiency gate. Together, these differently
gated results narrow the explanatory space: the text-only emoji-to-CLDR
lexical relation is repeatable, but the preregistered sparse late-component
account is not supported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Emoji look like compact visual objects to readers, but a text language model
receives them as encoded Unicode scalars and tokenizer-dependent sequences.
That distinction matters for interpretation. A representation probe may
recover an official label because the label co-occurs with the character in
standards, documentation, or training data. Such a result need not imply
rendered-glyph perception, a human-like concept, or a localized semantic
circuit.

We therefore ask a narrower two-part question. First, does a raw atomic emoji
retrieve a lexically disjoint CLDR keyword better than its literal
\texttt{U+...} spelling after exact token-count matching? Second, if this
observational relation is reliable, can a frozen low-rank late-component
intervention identify a representation that is both necessary and sufficient
for it? We do not claim that models ``understand emoji.'' The contribution is
an auditable separation between a broad lexical readout and one simple
mechanistic account.

The study is organized around three safeguards and their corresponding
results:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A four-view, three-bank protocol separates raw Unicode surface,
  official English CLDR name, a word-disjoint CLDR keyword, and literal
  codepoint spelling. The causal source and evaluation banks are held out from
  T19-L, although their surfaces may have appeared in studies before T19.
  \item The preregistered observational primary passes: 4/4 core cells and 9/9
  total cells are positive in unrestricted and exact four-view token-count
  matched analyses. The cross-model partial-conjunction value is $0.0027$.
  \item A conditionally admitted causal experiment passes all zero-hook and
  restoration controls but finds no primary block-24 attention or MLP output
  that is jointly necessary and sufficient. A secondary block-27 MLP patch is
  sufficient-only and is not a complete mediator.
\end{itemize}

The observational result extends beyond exact-short-name retrieval under the
frozen endpoint, while the causal result does not support one preregistered
rank-8 late-component mechanism. Neither result identifies which of the remaining
distributed, redundant, nonlinear, or item-private accounts is correct.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Emoji as lexical objects.}
Prior work learns emoji embeddings from descriptions
\citep{eisner2016emoji2vec} and builds sense inventories from lexical and
usage resources \citep{wijeratne2016emojinet}. We instead treat CLDR names and
keywords \citep{unicode2026cldr} as fixed, source-auditable endpoints for
probing frozen hidden states. The goal is not to produce a better emoji
embedding, but to test which textual relation is recoverable from a raw
Unicode input and which alternative explanations remain.

\paragraph{Probing and tokenizer controls.}
Probe accuracy can reflect properties of the control task rather than the
target representation \citep{hewitt2019designing}; tokenization itself can
materially change language-model comparisons \citep{rust2021tokenizer}. We
avoid a learned diagnostic classifier, use item-wise retrieval, and require
both unrestricted and exact four-view token-count-matched tests. This is a
token-count control, not token-identity matching or tokenizer independence.
Our readout is also more local than whole-representation similarity analyses
such as CKA \citep{kornblith2019similarity}: it asks whether the correct item
is the nearest cross-view partner.

\paragraph{Causal intervention.}
Causal mediation and activation interventions can distinguish readable
correlates from components that affect model computation
\citep{vig2020causal,meng2022rome,turner2023activation}. We adopt that
distinction but require a frozen site to pass both random-adjusted necessity
and sufficiency on a disjoint evaluation bank. The negative result is retained
rather than followed by an adaptive search over layers, ranks, or endpoints.

\section{Prospective design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Claim boundary, views, and banks}

For auditability, we retain two internal study labels. \textbf{T19-L}
denotes the observational lexical-retrieval arm (``L'' for lexical), and
\textbf{T19-C} denotes the conditionally admitted component-causal arm.
They are labels for the two arms defined below; no knowledge of the preceding
project sequence is required. Appendix \ref{app:lineage} gives the short
decision lineage that led to T19-L without treating earlier exploration as
part of its confirmatory evidence.

Throughout, \emph{glyph} means the raw Unicode scalar string supplied to a
text model. No raster image is rendered. Atomic emoji are single-codepoint,
fully-qualified Emoji 17 entries introduced no later than Emoji 13.1
\citep{unicode2025emoji}. For each item, the canonical view is the exact
English CLDR 48.2 \texttt{tts} value; the alias is one exact English CLDR
\texttt{default} keyword. After lowercasing and extracting ASCII
\texttt{[a-z0-9]+} words, canonical and alias word sets must be nonempty and
disjoint. The fourth view is the uppercase literal codepoint spelling.
When several keywords qualify, aliases are assigned by one deterministic
maximum bipartite matching with globally unique labels; a frozen SHA-256 edge
order breaks ties before any T19 model forward.

\subsection{Observational readout}

For each item and view, the frozen harness appends one of two suffix carriers
(\texttt{" a"} or \texttt{" |"}) at distances 1, 2, 4, and 8 and records the
hidden state at the selected suffix token at nine normalized depths. Batch
size is one; no chat template or added special token is used. The primary
late window is fixed at depths $0.75$, $0.875$, and $1.0$, yielding 24
contexts per item.

Within each view and context, hidden states are centered across items and each
row is L2-normalized. Let $z^{v,c}_i$ denote the resulting vector for item $i$,
view $v$, and context $c$. Cross-view top-1 retrieval is

\begin{equation}
  R_c(g \rightarrow v) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \mathbf{1}\left[i = \arg\max_j
  \left\langle z^{g,c}_i, z^{v,c}_j \right\rangle\right].
  \label{eq:retrieval}
\end{equation}

The per-model primary estimate is

\begin{equation}
  \Delta_m = \frac{1}{24}\sum_c
  \left[R_c(g \rightarrow \alias) -
  R_c(g \rightarrow \codepoint)\right]
  \label{eq:primary}
\end{equation}

on the 55 observational emoji. This is a nearest-neighbor readout from hidden
states, not a next-token generation score.

\subsection{Token-count intersection and cross-model test}

Every model must pass two components. The unrestricted component uses all 55
emoji and permutes alias correspondence within glyph kind. The matched
component retains non-singleton strata sharing the exact four-view
token-count signature and permutes aliases only within stratum. Retrieval in
that component uses the retained pool ($n=31$--$41$ emoji across models), so
its chance level is $1/n$; unrestricted and matched magnitudes are not treated
as directly commensurable. Canonical and codepoint correspondences stay fixed.
Thus the one-sided randomization test asks whether the frozen alias mapping
beats mappings allowed by its null; because the codepoint term is fixed, it is
not a paired significance test of alias--codepoint equality. The registered
primary combines that test with the observed comparative requirement
$\Delta_m\geq0.02$ in both components. At $n=55$, the latter requires at least
two net top-1 successes; in the smaller matched pools it requires at least one.
Each component uses 19,999 draws, and the model intersection-union $p$-value
is the maximum of the two component values.

The sole cross-model primary uses four frozen core cells: Llama 3.2 3B base,
Qwen2.5 7B base, OLMo 2 1B base, and Gemma 2 2B IT. It tests the partial
conjunction that at least $r=3$ of $m=4$ cells pass
\citep{benjamini2008partial}. With ordered model IUT values
$p_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq p_{(4)}$, the registered value is
$p_{\mathrm{PC}}=\min(1,2p_{(3)})$. The other five cells and all symbol or
canonical-name results are secondary and cannot open the causal gate.

We report percentile intervals from 20,000 item bootstrap replicates as
descriptive uncertainty. They are not substituted for the permutation-based
primary decision.

\subsection{Conditionally admitted causal test}

After the observational gate was committed, T19-C ran in the frozen Llama 3.2
3B base cell. At each of six sites (attention and MLP component outputs at
blocks 21, 24, and 27), we learned a rank-8 basis from the left singular
directions of the centered, row-normalized glyph-alias cross-covariance on 128
source emoji. A deterministic rank-8 control basis was drawn from the same
source span and made orthogonal to the learned basis. Blocks 24 attention and
MLP were primary; blocks 21 and 27 were secondary.

On 192 different evaluation emoji, necessity removes learned coordinates from
the glyph component output. Sufficiency adds the same-item learned
glyph-minus-codepoint coordinates to the codepoint output. Analogous operations
in the random basis define adjusted effects:

\begin{align}
N_s &= E_s(\text{random removal})-E_s(\text{learned removal}),\\
S_s &= E_s(\text{learned patch})-E_s(\text{random patch}),
\end{align}

where $E_s$ is the final-hidden alias-minus-codepoint top-1 endpoint. A site
must have both effects and both lower 95\% item-bootstrap bounds above $0.02$,
pass both one-sided sign-flip tests (99,999 draws), pass zero and
remove-restore engineering controls, and survive Holm correction across the
two primary sites \citep{holm1979sequential}. No site, rank, depth, carrier,
subset, or endpoint may be replaced after seeing the result.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A lexical relation beyond the official short name}

The registered cross-model primary passed. All four core cells were positive;
the third-smallest core IUT value was $0.00135$, yielding
$p_{\mathrm{PC}}=2(0.00135)=0.0027$. All nine frozen cells were positive and
exceeded the registered $0.02$ effect-size floor in both unrestricted and matched analyses
(Figure \ref{fig:model-effects}; exact values in Appendix
\ref{app:observational}). Model IUT values ranged from $0.00005$ to $0.00185$.
An independent implementation reconstructed all 36 glyph-kind by component
randomization tests and the same gate decision.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/t19_model_effects.pdf}
  \caption{Emoji alias-minus-codepoint top-1 effects in the registered roster
  order. Bars are descriptive 95\% item-bootstrap intervals (20,000
  replicates); the randomization tests determine the primary decision. Every
  point estimate exceeds the registered $0.02$ floor in unrestricted and
  exact token-count-matched analysis.}
  \label{fig:model-effects}

Across models, mean emoji alias effects were $+0.0979$ unrestricted and
$+0.1084$ matched. Mean canonical-name effects were larger, $+0.1440$ and
$+0.1718$. Thus, retrieval extends beyond the exact official name, although
the official name remains the stronger key on average. The alternative label
is an exact CLDR keyword, not a human-rated synonym or paraphrase;
training-data co-occurrence could still fully explain the relation.

Depth was secondary. The nine recorded fractions were $0, 0.125,\ldots,1.0$;
only $0.75$, $0.875$, and $1.0$ formed the primary window. Across models and
contexts, the unrestricted contrast was $+0.0409$ at depth 0, then $+0.0864$,
$+0.1152$, and $+0.0922$ at the three primary depths, whose mean is the
$+0.0979$ reported above. Individual cells had different onset and peak
depths, so these data do not support one universal trajectory.

\subsection{A named-symbol control limits the emoji-specific claim}

Non-emoji symbols showed mean alias effects of $+0.0236$ unrestricted and
$+0.0153$ matched, compared with $+0.0979$ and $+0.1084$ for emoji. The
emoji-minus-symbol point estimate was positive in all nine cells in both
components. Yet symbol aliases were heterogeneous: Qwen2.5 3B and 7B were
negative in both components. Canonical-name retrieval exceeded alias retrieval
for symbols in 9/9 cells in both components, with mean symbol canonical effects
of $+0.0750$ and $+0.0821$ (Figure \ref{fig:symbol-boundary}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/t19_emoji_symbol_boundary.pdf}
  \caption{Registered secondary comparison. Each row shows unrestricted (U)
  and token-count-matched (M) effects for the same frozen model. Official-name
  retrieval is not exclusive to emoji, while disjoint-keyword effects are
  stronger and more consistent for the frozen emoji panel. Glyph category,

  \label{fig:symbol-boundary}

This comparison cannot establish an emoji-specific faculty. The panels are
not matched on training frequency, lexical specificity, Unicode category,
visual familiarity, or token identity. Its role is to prevent the stronger
but false inference that only emoji acquire readable names.

\subsection{No frozen late-component site passes both causal gates}

Before intervention, the final-hidden glyph endpoint on the 192-item causal
evaluation bank was $E_s=0.07292$ at every site (alias top-1 $0.07292$;
codepoint top-1 $0$). All six causal sites then passed their engineering
checks: zero hooks and
remove-restore conditions reproduced the baseline final hidden state with
exactly zero RMS difference. Nevertheless, neither primary block-24 site
passed. Block-24 attention had adjusted necessity $0.0000$ and sufficiency
$-0.00521$; block-24 MLP had $+0.00521$ and $0.0000$. Their site IUT values
were $1.0$ and $0.74912$, respectively. None of the six sites passed both
components (Figure \ref{fig:causal}; exact values in Appendix
\ref{app:causal}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/t19_causal_sites.pdf}
  \caption{Random-adjusted causal effects with descriptive 95\% item-bootstrap
  intervals over 192 emoji held out from T19-L. Shaded rows and asterisks mark the two

  registered $0.02$ effect-size floor. No site has both necessity and sufficiency above the
  floor with positive lower bounds.}
  \label{fig:causal}

The secondary block-27 MLP patch produced adjusted sufficiency $+0.07812$
(95\% interval $[+0.03646,+0.11979]$, one-sided uncorrected
$p=0.00009$ across 12 component tests), but necessity
was $-0.01562$ (interval $[-0.04167,+0.00521]$, $p=0.96895$). The site IUT was
therefore $0.96895$. Moreover, the learned patch raised alias top-1 from
$0.0052$ to $0.0208$ while lowering codepoint self-retrieval from $1.0000$ to
$0.9375$. It is an incomplete perturbation effect, not clean alias insertion
or a necessary mechanism.

\section{Discussion}

The observational and causal results answer different questions. T19-L shows
that, under a fixed text-only readout, the same item is recoverable across raw
emoji and a lexically disjoint CLDR keyword more reliably than across raw emoji
and a literal codepoint string. Exact token-count matching makes a pure length
account insufficient. The symbol panel shows that official-name readout is a
broader Unicode phenomenon, while keyword breadth is graded rather than
categorically emoji-only.

T19-C does not support a tempting mechanistic shortcut: a shared rank-8 late
attention or MLP component, learned from one bank and tested on another, does
not pass joint necessity and sufficiency at the frozen sites. This failure does not adjudicate
the observational measurement; the two arms test different claims. It instead
shows that aggregate linear readability does not entail one sparse causal
mediator. Distributed, redundant, nonlinear, and item-private accounts remain
possible, but the present data do not distinguish among them.

The block-27 MLP asymmetry sharpens that distinction. A patch can move an
endpoint without identifying the normal route that produces it. Requiring
necessity, sufficiency, random-subspace adjustment, restoration, and a disjoint
evaluation bank prevents that isolated patch success from being upgraded to a
circuit claim.

\section{Limitations and broader impact}

\paragraph{Measurement.}
CLDR keywords are standardized annotations, not human judgments of synonymy,
natural naming, specificity, or familiarity. Retrieval is nearest-neighbor
geometry at selected suffix tokens, not generation or task behavior. Inputs
are Unicode text; no rendered glyph is perceived. Exact token-count matching
does not match token identities or equalize tokenizer construction.

\paragraph{Generalization.}
The nine model/tokenizer cells are a fixed, convenience-constrained roster
spanning several families and sizes \citep{yang2024qwen25,groeneveld2024olmo,
dubey2024llama3,gemmateam2024gemma2,jiang2023mistral,smollm2modelcard}. Model
family, parameter count, training data, tokenizer, and instruction tuning are
confounded, so the study supports neither a scale law nor a model-population
estimate. Exact emoji surfaces are not pretraining-novel. The symbol panel is
repository-text-fresh but not a pretraining holdout and is not exchangeable
with the emoji panel.

\paragraph{Mechanism.}
The negative causal result covers one Llama 3.2 3B checkpoint, rank 8, one
carrier and distance, two primary and four secondary sites, and one
final-hidden endpoint. Its exact engineering gates verify hook fidelity, not
power: no frozen positive intervention control calibrates the dynamic range at
the two primary sites. It does not show that no causal representation exists.
A future study should preregister such a positive control, distributed
multi-site removal, nonlinear or sparse readouts, and a new source/evaluation
split rather than selecting a method on the present evaluation bank.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and impact.}
The anonymous compact archive binds configurations, selections, summaries,
tests, validators, license notices, and the rendered paper, but omits
model-dependent raw arrays retained in execution scratch. Exact acquisition
duration and peak memory were not receipt-bound and are not reconstructed
after the fact. Better Unicode auditing could reveal hidden representation
failures in multilingual or social-media systems. Conversely, treating CLDR
correspondence as a person's meaning, sentiment, culture, or identity could
erase contextual variation and enable unjustified profiling. The measurements
should not be used for such inference. No personal data, human subjects, or
user messages are used.

\section{Conclusion}

Across nine frozen model/tokenizer cells, raw atomic emoji retrieve
word-disjoint CLDR keyword aliases better than literal codepoints after exact
four-view token-count matching. A named-symbol control bounds the specificity
of that observation, and a separately banked causal experiment finds no
primary rank-8 late component that is jointly necessary and sufficient. The
two results narrow different parts of the explanation: broad lexical
readability is repeatable, but it should not be mistaken for visual
understanding or evidence for a simple localized mechanism.

\section{Exploration path and decision lineage}
\label{app:lineage}

T19-L is the nineteenth internal study label in a longer audit trail, not a
concept that readers are expected to know in advance. The concise lineage
below includes only results that changed the next design decision. These
studies motivated T19-L, but they are not pooled with its preregistered
primary, and their exploratory or failed-confirmation status is unchanged.

\paragraph{Initial screening and token controls (baseline and Milestone 2).}
A colored-shape steering screen produced 25 positive results among 36
layer--seed--dose cells, but an exact tokenization-matched follow-up supported
one tested layer contrast and not the other. We therefore made tokenization
part of the estimand, retained negative cells, and stopped treating a pooled
steering score as one stable semantic effect.

\paragraph{Lexical exploration and failed reserves (T9--T12).}
A held-out Mistral cell first supported name-over-codepoint retrieval. A
nine-cell follow-up retained statistical signal but failed its registered
cross-model effect-size rule. Broader glyph-system exploration then suggested
Unicode-composition hypotheses, but neither survived testing on separately
reserved items. We kept both failures, dropped the generic composition claim,
and returned to a narrower lexical question.

\paragraph{Readable responses and unsuccessful causal tests (T15--T16).}
An emoji--word response pattern replicated prospectively, and frozen
forced-choice associations were detectable. Yet a subspace intervention and
a multi-layer transplantation each failed their stronger causal gates. This
separated readout from implementation and motivated the present joint
necessity--sufficiency criterion.

\paragraph{Depth mapping and fresh confirmation (T17--T18).}
Across 292 previously exposed atomic emoji, name-minus-codepoint retrieval was
positive in all nine model cells at middle-to-late depths. Peaks were
model-specific, and final item retrieval (9/9) did not require consistent
whole-representation CKA alignment (4/9 model cells). We therefore froze a
common late window, replaced the population, and made no shared-transition or
universal-manifold claim. On 192 repository-fresh composite emoji, the
official-name contrast then
passed in 4/4 core cells and 8/9 cells overall after exact token-count
matching. This justified asking whether the relation extends beyond the
official short name, while adding a named non-emoji control and reserving new
banks for causality.

\paragraph{T19-L and T19-C (current confirmatory and conditional arms).}
T19-L replaces the sole label endpoint with a word-disjoint CLDR keyword and
adds literal-codepoint and named-symbol boundaries. T19-C tests the frozen
rank-8 component account on disjoint source and evaluation banks. The present
paper reports the resulting pair of differently gated results: broad lexical
retrieval passes, while the simple late-component mediator does not.

This lineage explains why the final design contains four text views, exact
token-count strata, a non-emoji symbol panel, three disjoint item banks, and a
necessity--sufficiency intersection. It also explains what is absent: no
favorable prior layer, glyph system, or causal site was promoted into the
T19-L primary. Only that frozen primary supports the paper's confirmatory
lexical claim.

\section{Execution, licensing, and artifact boundary}
\label{app:artifact}

Both observational and causal acquisition were frozen to a Transformers/CUDA
BF16 backend and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090. Model artifacts, tokenizer replay
receipts, selection files, analysis summaries, randomization seeds, bootstrap
seeds, and validators are checksum-bound. Figure inputs in the paper are only
the compact validated model-cell and causal-site JSON summaries; a generated
manifest hashes both inputs and every plotted PDF/PNG output.

The anonymous evidence archive is a deterministic whitelist-only zip. Its
manifest records every payload path, byte count, and SHA-256; a validator
rejects unsafe paths, author or local-path tokens, model-weight formats, and
raw array formats. The archive includes the paper, compact results, selected
banks, code, validation receipts, and paired license/compute/ethics reports.
It omits model-dependent activation arrays, learned causal subspaces, the full
intervention ledger, model weights, and tokenizer files. Those raw scientific
objects remain bound by run receipts in execution scratch. The package thus
supports compact-summary and figure audit, not self-contained raw replay.

The observational acquisition used 7,920 batch-one forwards and produced
285,120 readout vectors. Causal acquisition used 256 source forwards, 2,880
evaluation forwards, and 8,064 intervention rows. No training or generation
was performed. Exact wall-clock time, peak memory, energy use, and total
exploratory compute were not preserved in the T19 compact receipts; the
compute checklist therefore remains negative rather than substituting older
run timings.
